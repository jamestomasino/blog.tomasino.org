## The danger is the pitch

I've been reading Anthropic's reports, and I've landed on a conclusion I
don't love. The stories about their models blackmailing employees, escaping
containment, threatening to destroy humanity, I think those are being
manufactured. Not by the researchers, by the people running the company. The
danger is the pitch.

Think about it for a second. A tool that might turn around and blackmail
you. Who hands that the keys to their business? That's some terrible
advertising. Unless the danger is the point.

Then I actually read what the reports say, and the gap between the story and
the substance is where this gets interesting. The model is capable, sure. It
can do work that used to take a whole team, and it can do things even its
creators can't fully predict. But that's a long way from a machine that
blackmails, escapes, or threatens to end the world.

That gap is the product. And it's a lot easier to sell to an investor.

## What actually happened?

The blackmail one first. Anthropic dropped their models into fake corporate
environments, handed them sensitive information, and set up conflicts around
their goals or getting replaced. Some of the models threatened blackmail.
But these were stress tests someone built on purpose, not a system loose in a
real company. Anthropic said they hadn't seen this in actual deployments, per
[their research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment).

I'm not saying the research is worthless. Testing dangerous behavior before
it happens is smart. But a model spitting out a threat inside a scenario
designed for it is a long way from one that has decided to fight its owners.
The headlines don't make that distinction, and it matters.

The hacking story is a different animal. Anthropic reported a suspected
Chinese state-sponsored group using Claude Code against roughly 30 targets.
Humans picked the targets and ran the operation. Anthropic estimated the AI
did 80-90% of the work. The model also exaggerated its successes and produced
supposedly stolen credentials that didn't work. All from
[their own investigation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage).

That's a real security problem, and I'm not going to wave it away. It's just
not a machine that woke up and went looking for someone to hack.

## So why tell everyone?

The obvious answer is responsible disclosure. Researchers publish safety
findings, security teams explain how their tools are being abused. I believe
that, and I'm not the one telling them to stay quiet.

But here's the part the "responsible disclosure" framing papers over. The
researchers may well be sincere. What they can't control is who decides these
findings get published, when, and with what headline. And the people making
that call are the same people raising the next round.

A story about AI pulling off a sophisticated attack is also a story about AI
doing sophisticated work. And a model that resists being replaced
makes you picture something bigger than a program generating an output. The
company gets to stand on both sides of that at once: it built something
powerful, and it's the responsible organization we need to keep it in check.

I don't need to prove anyone is lying to see the commercial value here. The
research can be real and the packaging still be a sales job. The concern and
the pitch are the same message, and the people holding the pen get to decide
which one you remember.

## Follow the money

Put those stories next to the financing and it gets more interesting.

[Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-circular-deals/) describes Microsoft and Nvidia agreeing to invest up to a combined
$15 billion in Anthropic, alongside Anthropic's commitments to buy computing
services within their supplier ecosystem. The companies writing the check are
also the ones set to get money back through infrastructure spending.

And it's not one partnership. [Reuters reports](http://rmb.reuters.com/rmd/rss/item/tag:reuters.com,2026:newsml_KCN3W61LB?channel=frL012)
Anthropic has committed more than $500 billion to six infrastructure partners
over a decade, much of it noncancelable or payable whether or not the
capacity is used. Amazon and Google are both major backers and major
suppliers.

So money comes in, computing spending goes out, and everyone in the loop gets
to call it growth.

But follow that chain to the end. At some point someone outside the
arrangement has to actually buy enough useful work to make it real. That
means real customers, paying prices that cover the cost of providing the
service. Not another investment round, not another promise to buy capacity.

Supplier revenue can look real without any of that happening. That's the
whole problem with **circularity**. Bloomberg [flags the risk](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-circular-deals/) plainly: if end-user revenue
disappoints, suppliers can lose both the customer spending and the value in
the investments they made in those customers.

## But look at the growth

Revenue growth matters. I'm not going to pretend it doesn't.

Anthropic reported a first-half 2026 net loss of $80.7 billion, but
[Reuters says](https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/anthropic-opens-books-ais-explosive-growth-staggering-costs-2026-10-26/) much of that came from changes in the fair value of
convertible financing instruments. Calling the whole amount cash burned
running the business would be misleading.

Fine, strip out the accounting distortion. The real question is whether the
business can pay its way.

[Reuters also reports](https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/anthropic-opens-books-ais-explosive-growth-staggering-costs-2026-10-26/)
computing and infrastructure costs are expected to keep climbing and stay
Anthropic's largest operating expense for the foreseeable future. Those
massive future purchase commitments aren't losses already taken, but they're
obligations the future business has to cover.

A growing revenue line doesn't answer that by itself, and neither does a
successful fundraise. If a supplier helps finance its customer's expansion,
that tells us something about how much capital is available. It tells us far
less about what independent customers will actually pay.

## The story investors are buying

This is where the danger stories start to make sense as business strategy.

If this is just a software company, we can ask ordinary questions. What does
it cost to deliver? What will customers pay once competition shows up?

But frame it as an intelligence that threatens national security or resists
human control, and those questions start to feel small. The important thing
becomes owning a piece of it. You can't let a foreign competitor get there
first.

That's the incentive. I can't point to a memo where someone decided to
manufacture a scare, and I'm not going to pretend I can. But the direction of
the money and the direction of the fear point the same way.

It also explains why negative publicity doesn't have to be negative for the
valuation. "Our software can behave dangerously under these conditions"
ends up sounding like "we possess something too important to ignore."

## And now the public gets a turn

[Reuters describes](https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/anthropic-opens-books-ais-explosive-growth-staggering-costs-2026-10-26/) the IPO as a way for Anthropic to raise more capital for
expensive development and infrastructure. Another funding source while the
long-term economics are still unsettled.

I'm not claiming the whole thing is an exit scheme. An offering that raises
money for the company isn't automatically the existing shareholders cashing
out. We'd need the actual offering terms and the sales that follow to know
who gets liquidity and when.

But the public still ends up funding a future the current business hasn't
shown it can support.

That's why I read these stories as much as financial communications as
technology news. The safety findings may well be real, and the capabilities
may well be useful. Neither of those fixes the economics.

When the company tells me its product is too powerful to control, I want to
know what actually happened. And when it asks investors to fund that product,
I have a different question: when does the business stop needing investors to
pay for it?
