I've been reading reports from the frontier AI companies, and I've increasingly
come to a conclusion that's deeply troubling. All the stories about their models
blackmailing employees, escaping containment, threatening to destroy humanity,
I think they are being manufactured intentionally by the people running the
company as a form of market manipulation.

Think about it for a second. You've built a tool that might turn around and
blackmail you. Who hands that the keys to their business, or hinges their own
growth on something inherently against your own plans. And second, why would you
tell everyone? That's some terrible advertising… unless that's the point.

When I actually read what the reports say the gap between the media story and
the substantive detail is interesting. The AI model is capable. It can do work
that used to take a whole team. It can do things even its creators can't fully
predict when prompted. But that's a long way from a machine that blackmails,
escapes, or threatens to end the world.

## What actually happened?

Lets look at the blackmail story first. Anthropic dropped their models into fake
corporate environments, handed them sensitive information, and set up conflicts
around their goals or getting replaced. Some of the models threatened blackmail.
But these were stress tests someone built on purpose, not a system loose in
a real company. Anthropic said explicitly that they hadn't seen this in actual
deployments, per [their research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment).

I'm not saying the research is worthless. Testing dangerous behavior before
it happens is smart. But a model spitting out a threat inside a scenario
designed for it is a long way from one that has decided to fight its owners.
The AI model did what it was instructed to do just like normal. The headlines
don't make that distinction. In fact, they sensationalize the inferred idea that
the model is deciding things on its own as a means to suggest consciousness.

The hacking story is a different animal. Anthropic reported a suspected
Chinese state-sponsored group using Claude Code against roughly 30 targets.
Humans picked the targets and ran the operation. Anthropic estimated the AI
did 80-90% of the work. The model also exaggerated its successes and produced
supposedly stolen credentials that didn't work. You can see the details from
[their own investigation](https://www.anthropic.com/news/disrupting-AI-espionage).

There's a real security problem being demonstrated from LLMs, and I'm not going
to suggest otherwise here. But that's a vastly different situation than what is
being implied in the media again. We're being influenced to think of this as
a machine that woke up and went looking for someone to hack.

## But why?

The bullshit canned answer is responsible disclosure. Researchers publish safety
findings, security teams explain how their tools are being abused. They should
be doing that. But these disclosures are working overtime as PR and marketing.

A story about AI pulling off a sophisticated attack is also a story about AI
doing sophisticated work. And a model that resists being replaced
makes you picture something bigger than a program generating an output. The
company gets to stand on both sides of that at once: it built something
powerful, and it's the responsible organization we need to keep it in check.

What better way to pull up the ladder behind you than to suggest this technology
is so dangerous it needs regulation, while position yourself as the only one
with the capability to regulate?

## Follow the money

The maneuvering doesn't end there, either. If you put these stories next to the
financing and it gets more interesting.

First, [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-ai-circular-deals/)
reporting describes Microsoft and Nvidia agreeing to invest up to a combined $15
billion in Anthropic, alongside Anthropic's commitments to buy computing
services within their supplier ecosystem. The companies writing the check are
also the ones set to get money back through infrastructure spending.

And it's not just one partnership. [Reuters
reports](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/anthropics-518-billion-ai-buildout-hinges-largely-on-deals-that-cannot-be-canceled-filing-shows-4922031)
Anthropic has committed more than $500 billion to six infrastructure partners
over a decade, much of it noncancelable or payable whether or not the capacity
is used. Amazon and Google are both major backers and major suppliers.

So money comes in, then flows right back to the investors, despite the money
going nowhere. But follow that chain to the end. At some point someone outside
the arrangement has to actually buy enough useful work to make it real. That
means real customers, paying prices that cover the cost of providing the
service. Not another investment shell game or promise to buy capacity.

Supplier revenue can look real without any of that happening. That's the whole
problem with **circularity**. Bloomberg flagged that
risk plainly: if
end-user revenue disappoints, suppliers can lose both the customer spending and
the value in the investments they made in those customers.

## But look at the growth!

Anthropic reported a first-half 2026 net loss of $80.7 billion, but
[Reuters says](https://www.reuters.com/legal/transactional/anthropic-opens-books-ais-explosive-growth-staggering-costs-2026-10-26/) much of that came from changes in the fair value of
convertible financing instruments. Calling the whole amount cash burned
running the business would be misleading.

Fine, lets strip out the accounting distortion. The question remains whether the
business can actually pay its way out of the hole.

Reuters also reports
computing and infrastructure costs are expected to keep climbing and stay
Anthropic's largest operating expense for the foreseeable future. Those massive
future purchase commitments represent new obligations the business has to cover
on top of of their debts already incurred.

## The story investors are buying

This is where the danger stories start to make sense as business strategy.
If this is just a software company, we can ask ordinary questions. What does
it cost to deliver? What will customers pay once competition shows up? But frame
it as an intelligence that threatens national security or resists human control,
and those questions start to feel small. The important thing becomes owning
a piece of it. You can't let a foreign competitor get there first.

## And now…

Reuters describes the IPO as a way for Anthropic to raise more capital for
expensive development and infrastructure. Another funding source while the
long-term economics are still unsettled.

I'm not claiming the whole thing is an exit scheme (despite my fears it might
be). An offering that raises money for the company isn't automatically the
existing shareholders cashing out. We'd need the actual offering terms and the
sales that follow to know who gets liquidity and when. 

I still think their ultimate goal will be to get picked up by the US government
and made essential to national security. That frees them from the shackles of
profit. In the meantime the public still ends up funding a future the current
business hasn't shown it can support.
